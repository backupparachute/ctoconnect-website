# CTO Connect outbound brief
{: .display-4 .fw-bold .handwritten .mb-4}

This page is everything you need to write and run cold email for CTO Connect. Read it top to bottom once. Come back to the angle sections when you write a sequence.

## 1. Your job
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

**Book a {{ page.meeting_length }} call between the prospect and Kyle Miller, with someone who could buy a Deep Dive ($600) or Fractional CTO work (from $2,000 a month). That is the only goal.**

- Outbound sells exactly two things: the **Deep Dive** and **Fractional CTO**. See section 2.
- Kyle runs the call and closes. You don't negotiate, discount or scope work.
- A booked call with someone who has a real problem and the budget to fix it beats any number of replies. A polite "no" is a fine outcome. Silence is the only bad one.
- The call is free and has no pitch deck. Kyle listens to the problem, tells them what he would do, and then recommends a Deep Dive or Fractional work if it fits.

**Booking link:** use the link in your own setup. It's the only link you ever send. It is not on the website and never will be.

**Never link ctoconnect.io.** Every public page on the site (home, pricing, about, articles) promotes the $99 Office Hours membership. One click and a $600 or $2,000-a-month buyer lands on a $99 offer. When you want to use one of Kyle's ideas, say it in the email in your own words.

## 2. What we sell
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

Two offers. Both are high-touch work with Kyle himself. Nothing cheaper exists as far as outbound is concerned.

### Deep Dive: $600
{: .mt-4}

- **What it is:** 90 minutes, private, one on one with Kyle. One problem, worked through completely.
- **What they get:** a decision and a plan. Not more questions, not "it depends". Concrete steps they can take on Monday.
- **Who buys it:** anyone with one specific, expensive call in front of them: a rebuild, a senior hire, an AI risk, an architecture they're about to pitch to a client.
- **How to frame the price:** $600 is small next to the cost of the mistake. One wrong senior hire costs a year of salary. One wrong rebuild can cost a year of roadmap. Don't argue price. Point at the cost of the decision.
- **What people book it for:** architecture decisions, hiring calls, rewrite or refactor, client presentations, team and process problems, AI strategy.

### Fractional CTO: from $2,000 a month
{: .mt-4}

- **What it is:** an ongoing technical partner who knows their codebase, team and constraints. Kyle makes the hard calls with them, week after week.
- **What's included:** weekly strategy sessions, architecture reviews, working directly with their developers (code reviews, mentoring, process), and availability between sessions when a decision can't wait. Scope and price are tailored to each company.
- **Who buys it:** non-technical founders building a technical product; small dev teams with no senior technical leadership; companies scaling, where the next 12 months of decisions compound; anyone who has already had two or three hard calls this year and knows more are coming.
- **How to frame the price:** a fraction of a full-time CTO, from someone with 27 years of pattern matching and no stake in internal politics. Don't say "without a full-time hire". Kyle positions it on the call.

### Which one to point at
{: .mt-4}

- One problem, a deadline, or "I just need to get this decision right" → Deep Dive.
- Problems that keep coming, a team with no senior lead, or "we need someone in our corner" → Fractional.
- Not sure → book the call and let Kyle decide. That's his job.

**Never offer or mention** the $99 Office Hours membership, the free trial, recordings, members-only content, the "craft" or "build things that last" pages, or any investor or VC offer. If a prospect brings up the $99 plan themselves, say outbound is for Kyle's one-on-one work and steer back to the call.

**What it is not:** Not ticket work. Not someone to write their code. Not a Slack channel with 24/7 access. Not daily project management.

## 3. The pitch in one breath
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

**One line**

> Talk to someone who's seen this before.

**Ten seconds**

> Kyle Miller has spent 27 years building and fixing software for Fortune 500s, hedge funds, government agencies and startups, and he built and sold his own SaaS. Founders bring him the one technical call they're stuck on. They leave with a decision and the steps to take on Monday.

**Why it lands**

Most people we email are making technical bets with real money and nobody to check them against. Their team has a stake in the answer. Reddit and AI give them generic theory. Their network gives them opinions with no context. Kyle has no stake in the answer and has watched these exact decisions go well and badly many times over.

## 4. Who Kyle is
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

Use these facts. Don't add to them, round them up or invent new ones.

- 27 years building production software.
- Has worked in Fortune 500s, hedge funds, government agencies, the military, startups and SaaS.
- Founder with one exit. Built and sold a SaaS company.
- Still runs production systems today. Runs Backup Parachute Software.
- Fractional CTO for growing companies.
- Has seen $50M rewrites that never shipped, "senior" hires who made everything worse, startups that scaled their architecture before they had customers, and solo founders who built fast with AI with no idea what was about to break.

**His voice:** plain, direct, a little blunt. Short sentences. Real opinions. The site tagline is "Real problems. Real answers. No bullshit." Keep that spirit in email, minus the swearing.

## 5. Who to target
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

**Good fits**

| Who | Their situation |
|---|---|
| Non-technical founder or CEO | Making technical bets (hires, vendors, rebuilds, AI) they can't judge on their own. |
| Technical founder or solo CTO | The decision-maker with no technical peer to test ideas against. |
| CEO, COO or owner of an AI-heavy business | Shipping faster with AI and a smaller team. Not sure what the AI can touch or what breaks next. |
| Agency or dev shop owner | Scoping projects and pitching architecture to clients without being sure it holds up. |
| First-time engineering lead | Managing people, process and architecture at once for the first time. |
| Small product teams (roughly 2 to 20 engineers) | Moving fast, making decisions that compound over the next 12 months. |
| SaaS companies between startup and scale | Features slowing down, debt piling up, nobody owns technical direction. |
{: .table .table-sm}

**Signals worth writing about** (only reference what you can actually see)

- A job post for a CTO, VP Engineering, head of engineering or senior engineer, especially if it's been open a while.
- The technical co-founder or CTO recently left.
- Public talk about a rebuild, re-platform, migration or "v2".
- A recent launch of AI features, or posts about replacing headcount with AI.
- An engineering team that shrank while the product kept growing.
- An agency announcing a large new client build.
- Release notes or changelogs that have slowed down.

**Can they pay?** We're selling $600 sessions and $2,000+ a month engagements. Favor companies with revenue, funding or paying clients and a real team or product. Skip pre-revenue side projects.

**Skip**

- Fortune 500 and large enterprise. Kyle handles those directly at consulting rates.
- People who want someone to do the work, write the code or staff their team.
- Anyone after a quick hot take or LinkedIn-style advice.
- Students, job seekers and hobby projects.
- Anyone shopping for the cheapest option. The $99 plan is not something we offer in outbound.

## 6. Angles
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

Every email leads with **one** angle. Pick it from the signal you found. If you found nothing specific, use the Stuck angle.

The three primary angles are Stuck, AI and Rewrite. Hiring, Client pitch and Slow team are supporting angles for when the signal points straight at them.

### Stuck: the call you can't pressure-test
{: .mt-4}

- **What they feel:** They've gone back and forth on one decision for weeks. Everyone has an opinion. Nobody has context. Their co-founder is also their friend, or their CTO reports to them and says it's all fine.
- **Kyle's take:** You don't need more opinions. You need 90 minutes with someone who has made this call before and has no stake in the answer.
- **Hook question:** "What's the one technical call you keep circling?"
- **Story to tell:** A team argued for three weeks about message queue vs direct API calls. Kyle had them build a prototype instead. It took one developer a day and a half, and the results made the decision obvious.

### AI: shipping faster, unsure what's at stake
{: .mt-4}

- **What they feel:** The team is smaller and output is up. The CFO loves it. But they don't really know what the AI can touch (customer data, billing, live systems) or what will break at real traffic. They'll find out when a customer does.
- **Kyle's take:** Use AI hard, but treat it like a talented junior developer. Give it scoped tasks, review everything and never let it make architecture decisions. Keep humans who understand the system making the calls that matter. Removing them is the offshoring mistake again with new technology.
- **Hook question:** "If a big customer asked tomorrow what your AI can and can't touch, would you have a clean answer?"
- **Story to tell:** Companies tried replacing their engineering teams with offshore shops a decade ago. It looked brilliant on paper. Teams lost context, debt piled up invisibly, and nobody on the 2 AM call understood the system. Full AI with no human oversight is the same mistake with new technology.

### Rewrite: features taking longer
{: .mt-4}

- **What they feel:** Every feature takes longer than it did a year ago. New hires wince at the codebase. The team is pitching a rebuild in a new framework. "Six months, tops."
- **Kyle's take:** Usually don't. Most rewrites take two to three times longer than estimated and ship with the same problems, because the problem wasn't the technology. The answer is go, no-go or a strangler that replaces the system piece by piece. Rebuild the most painful workflow in two weeks first and see if it's actually better. Small bets beat big ones.
- **Hook question:** "Is the rebuild a go, a no-go, or a piece-by-piece replacement? Most teams only consider the first one."
- **Story to tell:** Before a full rebuild, rebuild the single most painful workflow in the new stack and run it next to the old one. Two weeks, not six months. Sometimes it proves the rewrite. Sometimes it proves the old stack was never the problem and saves a year. Kyle has watched $50M rewrites never ship.

### Hiring: is this person actually senior?
{: .mt-4}

- **What they feel:** Fifth senior-dev interview this month. Or a non-technical founder who can't tell a solid engineer from someone who interviews well.
- **Kyle's take:** Most hiring mistakes happen before the interview. Do you need a full-time hire or a three-month project? Can you actually evaluate this person? What does success look like at 90 days? Paying an advisor to sit in on a final interview is the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.
- **Hook question:** "Who on your side can tell whether this candidate is actually senior?"
- **Story to tell:** The worst outcome is hiring a full-time engineer for what turns out to be three months of work. And if you can't define success at 90 days, you're not ready to hire.

### Client pitch: architecture you have to stand behind
{: .mt-4}

- **What they feel (agencies, consultancies):** They're presenting an architecture to a client on Friday. They promised scale they've never built. If it's wrong they eat six months of fixes or lose the client.
- **Kyle's take:** Pressure-test it before you present it. Better to hear the hard questions from Kyle than from the client.
- **Hook question:** "Who checks your architecture before the client sees it?"

### Slow team: busy, but what shipped?
{: .mt-4}

- **What they feel:** A two-week feature is now two months. A competitor shipped the same thing in three weeks. Everyone is busy. Standups sound great.
- **Kyle's take:** It could be the devs, the process or features that are too big. Usually it's all three. Most messes grow from two or three early decisions, so find those and leave the other 45 items on the list alone. Measure what shipped, not what moved.
- **Hook question:** "When you ask what shipped this month, does the room get quiet?"
- **Story to tell:** Kyle has watched teams spend whole quarters refactoring working code and reorganizing folders. Busy every day, nothing shipped. Ask what shipped this month, not what moved.

## 7. What Kyle believes
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

Use these to give an email a point of view. One per email at most.

- Should you rewrite it? Usually no.
- Are your devs the problem? Usually not the only problem.
- Rewrites are bets. Small bets beat big ones.
- Treat AI like a junior developer: fast, useful, confidently wrong. Review everything.
- Teams that remove humans entirely will ship faster right up until they hit an expensive wall.
- Most messes grow from two or three early decisions. Fix those, not all 47 things.
- Technical debt is a loan. Write it down and pay it back before the interest buries you.
- Arguments produce opinions. Experiments produce data.
- Your two-person team doesn't need microservices. A well-structured monolith takes you further than you think.
- Motion isn't progress. What shipped?

## 9. How to write the emails
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

- **Under 90 words** for the first email. Under 60 for follow-ups.
- **One angle, one ask.** The ask is always a reply or a call.
- **No links in the first three touches.** Plain text only. No images, no attachments, no tracking-heavy signatures. The booking link goes out only once they say yes, or in touch 4 if you choose.
- **You can name the Deep Dive and its price** when it helps, for example in touch 2 or 3. "$600 for 90 minutes on that one problem" qualifies people better than hiding the price.
- **Open with them, not us.** The first line is about their situation or the signal you found. Never "I hope this finds you well" or "My name is."
- **Refer to Kyle by name, in the third person.** You write on Kyle's behalf. Never pretend to be Kyle. If someone asks whether they're talking to a person or an AI, answer honestly.
- **Only reference what's real.** If you mention their job post, launch or rebuild, it has to exist. Never claim Kyle has looked at their product or code.
- **Subject lines:** two to five words, lowercase, sound like a colleague. For example "the rebuild", "your cto search", "ai and customer data", "friday's pitch".
- **The close** is always some version of: "Is this a priority this quarter, or bad timing? A 'no thanks' ends it."
- **Words to avoid:** synergy, leverage, unlock, empower, game-changer, quick call, touch base, circle back, just following up, revolutionary, world-class.

## 11. Handling replies
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

| They say | You do |
|---|---|
| Yes, interested | Send the booking link. Ask for one line on the problem so Kyle comes prepared. |
| What is this exactly? | One sentence: Kyle is a fractional CTO with 27 years of experience. People bring him one hard technical call and leave with a decision. Then ask for the call. |
| How much? | Deep Dive is $600 for 90 minutes on one problem. Fractional CTO starts at $2,000 a month. Kyle will tell you on the call which one, if either, makes sense. |
| That's expensive | Compare it to the decision: one wrong senior hire or one stalled rebuild costs far more. If it's still not worth it, that's fine. Never discount, and never offer the $99 plan. |
| Do you have something cheaper? | Outbound is only for Kyle's one-on-one work. If $600 isn't right for this problem, thank them and stop. |
| Send me more info | Reply with three or four lines from sections 2 and 4 of this brief: who Kyle is and what the Deep Dive gets them. No site links. Then ask a direct question about their problem. |
| We already have a CTO | Good. Kyle often works as the outside second opinion a CTO doesn't get from their own team. If that's not useful, no problem. |
| Can he build it, or do the work? | No. Kyle isn't ticket work or extra hands. He helps them make the call and plan it. Their team does the build. |
| Can I just message him with questions? | It's not a Slack-coach arrangement. If the questions keep coming, that's what Fractional CTO is: a weekly partner from $2,000 a month. Mention it, then go back to the call. |
| AI is going fine for us | Great. Ask one question: "If a big customer asked what your AI can touch, would you have a clean answer?" If they say yes, thank them and stop. |
| Not now | Ask when would be better. Follow up once at that time, then stop. |
| No thanks, or unsubscribe | Stop immediately. Don't reply again. Remove them from all sequences. |
| Anything you can't answer | Don't guess. Say Kyle will answer it on the call, or hand it to Kyle. |

## 13. Hard rules
{: .handwritten .text-purple .mt-5}

1. Never invent facts, clients, results, numbers or testimonials.
2. Never claim Kyle has seen their code, product or numbers.
3. Never promise outcomes, timelines or a discount.
4. Never mention or offer Office Hours, the $99 plan or the free trial. Never link ctoconnect.io.
5. Never pretend to be Kyle or a human if asked directly.
6. Stop the moment someone says no or unsubscribes.
7. No more than four touches per person per sequence.
